# Few agents, typed state and real verifiers win

The research points one way. For **harness-system-one**, the strongest default is a single ReAct-style tool loop with three additions: an explicit, typed "Orient" state with an act/ask/recover/stop gate, hard termination and progress controls, and concrete verification. Multi-agent fan-out should be reserved for read-heavy, parallelizable work, run by a deterministic orchestrator with roughly three to five isolated workers. Each framework in the question contributes something real, but less than its popular version promises. ReAct's lasting value is the loop with environment feedback, not the interleaved free-text "thought". Its interleaving and thought content proved fragile under ablation, and its context cost grows quadratically with steps. OODA's value is Boyd's emphasis on Orient as a persistent world model. The one LLM study that operationalizes it found that removing Orient caused the largest performance drop. "Faster loop wins" does not hold up, and as a decision procedure OODA has almost no controlled evidence behind it. The "two-pizza team" survives as an organizational lesson, but Amazon's own insiders reportedly concluded that headcount mattered less than a single-threaded owner of separable work. Controlled 2025–2026 agent studies agree: once agents must coordinate, the benefits peak at about 3–7 agents and turn negative when the single agent is already competent. At matched compute, self-consistency usually beats elaborate multi-agent systems. The positive cases share a recipe: expert-designed decomposition, code-driven orchestration, independent workers, and deterministic aggregation. Several figures below rest on abstracts, search snippets, or secondary summaries rather than full-text reads, and they are flagged where they appear.

## ReAct endures as a loop, not as a reasoning format

ReAct (Yao et al., ICLR 2023) added free-language "thoughts" to an agent's action space, alternating Thought, Action, and Observation. Its strongest evidence comes from interactive, long-horizon environments. With PaLM-540B, ReAct reached **71% success on ALFWorld (best of six prompts) versus 45% for action-only prompting and 37% for an imitation-learning baseline trained on 10^5 expert trajectories**, and on WebShop it lifted success rate from 30.1 to 40.0 ([ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)). Its knowledge-QA claim is weaker than often remembered. Alone, **ReAct scored 27.4 EM on HotpotQA versus 29.4 for chain-of-thought**. The best results came from hybrids that fall back between ReAct and CoT with self-consistency (35.1 EM), and these matched 21-sample CoT-SC with only 3–5 samples ([ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)). ReAct's real QA contribution was grounding. Hallucination accounted for 0% of its failures versus 56% for CoT, and CoT reached a correct answer from a hallucinated trace more than twice as often (14% vs 6% false positives). The authors' own error analysis also names the failure modes harnesses still fight: **47% of ReAct failures were reasoning errors, including repetitively regenerating previous thoughts and actions**, and 23% came from uninformative search results that "derail" the model ([ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)). Step caps, loop detection, and fallback to another strategy are therefore present in the very first ReAct paper.

The successors each modify one axis of the loop, and each buys its gains with a dependency a production harness may not have. Reflexion wraps ReAct in an outer loop that turns failures into verbal lessons. It solved **130 of 134 ALFWorld tasks** and reached 91.0 HumanEval pass@1 versus GPT-4's 80.1. However, its HotpotQA evaluator used the ground-truth answer as an oracle between trials, it gave no gain with a weaker model (starchat-beta), and it failed on WebShop, where the authors conclude it "is unable to solve tasks that require a significant amount of diversity and exploration" ([Reflexion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366)). Tree of Thoughts raised GPT-4 on Game of 24 from 4.0% (CoT) to 74%, but the authors estimate it can require **5–100× more generated tokens than CoT**, and its gains on GSM8K were marginal ([ToT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10601)). LATS runs Monte-Carlo tree search over ReAct trajectories and reached 0.63 HotpotQA EM versus ReAct's 0.32 with GPT-3.5. It used about **173k tokens per success** and requires the ability to revert the environment to earlier states ([LATS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04406)). ReWOO plans the whole tool sequence up front. On HotpotQA it cut tokens from 9,795 to 1,986 while slightly raising accuracy (40.8% to 42.4%), and when every tool returned "No evidence found" ReAct collapsed to zero while consuming *more* tokens. ReWOO degenerates, though, in environments with little prior information, where the plan cannot be fixed in advance ([ReWOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18323)). CodeAct makes executable Python the action space, lifting GPT-4 from 52.4% (JSON actions) to **74.4% success on multi-tool tasks with fewer turns**. The advantage disappears on atomic single calls, where JSON was better ([CodeAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01030)). Reported figures for CoT, Self-Consistency, Plan-and-Solve, Toolformer, and Self-Refine in the underlying notes come from abstracts as recalled, not re-read, so they are left out here.

Two lines of critique sharpen what actually matters. Verma, Bhambri, and Kambhampati (2024) perturbed only the exemplars on ALFWorld. **Moving all guidance into one up-front plan improved GPT-4 from 23.3% to 43.3%**, a placebo thought ("take a deep breath…") did about as well as real reasoning, and success collapsed to 0–6.6% when the exemplars came from a different task type. Their conclusion is that few-shot ReAct's performance reflects "approximate retrieval" from similar examples rather than reasoning ([Verma et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13966)). The study covers only few-shot prompting on ALFWorld with 2024 models. Modern harnesses use native function calling without exemplars, and no replication on frontier models was found. The second critique is security. A November 2026 Berkeley paper argues that ReAct "collaps[es] observation and action selection into a single inference step," so untrusted content arrives exactly when the next action is chosen. Its compile-then-execute alternative reached **77.8% utility with a 0% attack success rate on WebArena Verified, versus 66.7% and 16.2% for ReAct**. The cost was 327 s latency versus 87 s, and it cannot handle tasks whose operations come from untrusted content ([Baya](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06039)). The counterweight is that plain loops with good tools remain hard to beat. A zero-shot think→act→observe loop over a typed DSL hit **84.5% on BIRD versus 85.3% for the ~20-call CHESS pipeline, at 7.5 s versus 53.7 s median latency** ([ReAct-SQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22651)). Anthropic's guidance likewise defines agents as "LLMs using tools based on environmental feedback in a loop", with stopping conditions and the simplest workable design ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). Read together, the evidence says the loop with environment feedback is causal and the free-text thought format is much less so.

## OODA's value lies in Orient, not in speed

Boyd never wrote a book. The only full OODA diagram appears in his 1995–96 briefing "The Essence of Winning and Losing", held in the USMC University Boyd collection ([USMCU](https://www.usmcu.edu/Portals/218/HD/Digitized%20Collections/COLL_2062%20John%20Boyd/COLL%202062_John%20Boyd_Box%208/110129-The%20Essence%20of%20Winning%20and%20Losing%20Briefing.PDF)). In that diagram, Orientation is the hub. It is shaped by heritage, culture, experience, and analysis and synthesis, and through "implicit guidance & control" it drives Observe and Act directly, bypassing explicit decision. (The slide wording here comes via a search summary, and the OCR is garbled in places.) Scholars treat the popular four-box, "be faster" loop as a misreading. Chet Richards' review of Frans Osinga's *Science, Strategy and War* says the biggest misconception is that OODA is a sequential step model, and that Osinga confines the tempo logic mainly to the tactical level, with accurate orientation at least as important above it ([DNI review](https://dnipogo.org/dni_reviews/science_strategy_and_war/); [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/products/9780203088869)). The empirical base for OODA as a decision method is thin. Brehmer's 2005 Dynamic OODA critique, summarized by Simpson et al., argues the model omits "command concepts, planning, exit criteria or system delays" and "overly accentuates speed" ([arXiv 2109.06874](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.06874)). The researchers found no controlled study showing that using OODA improves decisions. That absence is meaningful but does not prove no such study exists.

The one rigorous LLM operationalization makes Orient concrete and measurable. **OODA-Tool** (arXiv preprint, August 2026) diagnoses a failure it calls "state-action competition": in ReAct and direct function calling, state tracking and next-call generation share one autoregressive trajectory, so "the pressure to produce the next call can overwrite or ignore information accumulated earlier" ([OODA-Tool](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24368)). Its four stages are typed and controller-checked. Observe rebuilds a provenance-aware task state. Orient picks one of five modes (solvable with a tool, need clarification, respond directly, recover from failure, done) and acts as a gate. Decide sets the call structure without argument values, and Act binds grounded arguments. On ToolDial with Qwen3 models from 0.6B to 14B, **task success rose from 65.43 to 85.10 at 0.6B and from 84.77 to 94.90 at 14B relative to ReAct-LoRA**, and ReAct-LoRA was worse than direct function calling at every scale. **Removing Orient caused the largest ablation drop.** Premature calls fell to 3.0% from 9.0%, and the gains concentrated on long-history, missing-information, and state-change turns. Three findings temper the result. Latency was **2.36× direct calling**. Gains shrank with model scale and transferred only modestly zero-shot. And a shared single adapter running four structured passes performed nearly as well as four specialized adapters, which means **the benefit comes from staging and typed interfaces, not from separate per-phase agents** ([OODA-Tool](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24368)). This is a single unreplicated preprint on small fine-tuned models, and no comparison on frontier models or coding benchmarks exists.

Security turns OODA's speed rhetoric on its head. Schneier and Raghavan's "Agentic AI's OODA Loop Problem" (*IEEE Security & Privacy*, Oct 2025) argues that agents embed untrusted actors inside their loops: "the speed of your OODA loop is irrelevant when the adversary controls your sensors and actuators." It also argues that compromises persist in conversation history and spread to other agents, and it frames a "fast, smart, secure: pick any two" trilemma ([Schneier blog](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2025/10/agentic-ais-ooda-loop-problem.html); [Harvard Cyber summary](https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2025-10/agentic-ais-ooda-loop-problem)). The schneier.com host was unreachable during research, so these quotes come from search summaries and should be spot-checked against the essay PDF. A conceptual Brazilian Armed Forces architecture maps roles onto OODA (situational analysis, decision support, countermeasure options) and puts a mandatory human-approval gate before any effect. It also cites A2ASecBench reporting attack success rates up to 100% against unguarded agent-to-agent systems ([arXiv 2609.20080](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.20080)). Taken together, the shared orientation state is both the most valuable component and the highest-value attack target in a multi-agent harness.

## Matched compute erases most multi-agent advantages

The 2023–2024 wave of frameworks established four architecture families: role/SOP pipelines (MetaGPT, ChatDev), debate (Du et al., Liang et al.), orchestrator-worker (AutoGen, Magentic-One), and graph or layered ensembles (GPTSwarm, Mixture-of-Agents). Most reported gains against single-call baselines without matching compute. The researchers could not re-fetch most of those papers' headline numbers, so they are not repeated here. Their lasting contributions are structural: structured documents in place of chat, ledgers for progress tracking, and independent proposals followed by aggregation.

Later controlled studies are much less flattering. MAST, from UC Berkeley, built a failure taxonomy from expert-annotated traces (human κ = 0.88) and released **1,642 traces across 7 frameworks**. It found failure rates of **41% to 86.7%** in open multi-agent systems ([MAST](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)). System-design issues made up 44.2% of failure annotations, inter-agent misalignment 32.3%, and verification 23.5%. The top single modes were **step repetition (15.7%), reasoning–action mismatch (13.2%), and unawareness of termination conditions (12.4%)**, and the last was "fatal", appearing almost only in failed runs. These are shares of failure annotations, not the share of runs that fail. Role disobedience was only 1.5%. Prompt and topology interventions improved ChatDev by just 9.4–15.6%, which the authors judged insufficient. On debate, a single agent with strong prompts and demonstrations matched the best multi-agent discussion, which won only when no demonstrations were given ([Rethinking the Bounds](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2402.18272)). InstaDeep found multi-agent debate "did not reliably outperform" self-consistency or ensembling, that it was highly sensitive to hyperparameters, and that tuned settings carried over to GPT-4 but not to Mixtral ([Should we be going MAD?](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2311.17371)). A NeurIPS 2025 paper attributes most debate gains to majority voting rather than discussion ([Debate or Vote](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.17536)), but that is recorded at abstract level only.

The 2026 audits are the most decisive. "The Illusion of Multi-Agent Advantage" pitted six automatically designed multi-agent systems against **CoT-SC with a five-sample vote**. CoT-SC often matched or beat them **at under 10% of their cost**. Half of AFlow's searched workflows collapsed to "run one prompt 3× then aggregate", and DyLAN's specialized role agents reached instant unanimous consensus 70–90% of the time ([Illusion](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.13003)). The same paper supplies the most important positive result. A **hand-designed Expert-MAS with a Python orchestrator dispatching parallel extractor and calculator agents, plus deterministic final comparison, raised GPT-5 from 57.0% to 96.5%** on a decomposable financial benchmark at cost comparable to CoT-SC. GPT-4o stayed bottlenecked by its base abilities, which the authors call a "competency floor" ([Illusion](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.13003)). "The Interaction Tax" ran 10 multi-agent configurations at identical budgets on 11 verifier-scored tasks, and **none reliably beat the best single-agent baseline**. Model diversity helped (+0.188), but exchanging full solutions erased that diversity within a single round, and the first critique round *degraded* graded solutions in 57% of runs. Critique paid off only when faults were locatable, such as capacity violations ([Interaction Tax](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.23541)). A Stanford study reporting that single agents beat multi-agent systems at equal thinking-token budgets points the same way, but it is known here only through secondary summaries and its main table has been publicly questioned ([arXiv 2604.02460](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02460)).

Practitioner evidence agrees once the cost is accounted for. Anthropic's research system, an Opus 4 lead with Sonnet 4 subagents, **beat single-agent Opus 4 by 90.2%** on an internal eval. However, **token usage alone explained 80% of BrowseComp variance**, and multi-agent runs cost about **15× the tokens of chat** ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). No compute-matched single-agent baseline was published, so that gain is best read as "more parallel test-time compute across separate context windows". Anthropic states that tasks needing shared context or many inter-agent dependencies, including "most coding tasks", are a poor fit. It also documents over-spawning (50 subagents for simple queries), duplicated work from vague delegation, and a synchronous lead that blocks on its slowest worker. Its fixes were explicit delegation briefs, effort-scaling rules, external memory for plans, and subagents that return lightweight references instead of full transcripts. Cognition's "Don't Build Multi-Agents" argues the same boundary from the other side: parallel writers make conflicting implicit decisions, so share full context and keep one thread for coupled work ([Biweekly Engineering summary](https://biweekly-engineering.beehiiv.com/p/the-case-against-multi-agents-biweekly-engineering-episode-42)). The original essay was unreachable, so this rests on secondary coverage. The two positions reconcile cleanly: fan out for read-heavy exploration, and keep a single writer for shared artifacts.

## Agent teams peak near three to seven, below two pizzas

The two-pizza rule was never just a headcount. AWS describes Bezos's rule as teams "no larger than can be fed by two pizzas" ([AWS whitepaper](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/introduction-devops-aws/two-pizza-teams.html)), though that text was seen only through a search snippet. Amazon paired the rule with end-to-end ownership, a per-team metric, and service-interface decoupling. Steve Yegge's unofficial "platform rant" recalls the c.2002 mandate that teams communicate only through service interfaces ([Yegge](https://gist.github.com/chitchcock/1281611)). Summaries of Bryar and Carr's insider book *Working Backwards* report that **Amazon later found team size was not a good predictor of success**. The leader's skills, authority, and singular focus mattered more, and the model evolved into "single-threaded leaders" heading "separable, largely autonomous" teams ([Keqiu summary](https://medium.com/keqius-management-notes/working-backwards-e3a8ea5a99ab); [del Gallego](https://pedrodelgallego.github.io/blog/amazon/single-threaded-model)). The book's primary text was not accessed, and the popular "6–10 people" figure has no official Amazon source.

Human team science explains why small teams win, and which reasons transfer to agents. Brooks's n(n−1)/2 communication paths mean **a 6-person team has 15 links and a 12-person team has 66**, and Hackman advises staying in single digits ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2009/05/why-teams-dont-work), not re-fetched). Hackman and Vidmar's self-report study put the optimum near 4.6 members ([Fortune](https://money.cnn.com/magazines/fortune/fortune_archive/2006/06/12/8379238/index.htm)). QSM's project data repeatedly favor small teams, with best-in-class projects using teams more than 4× smaller than the worst ([QSM](https://qsm.com/blog/2012/top-performing-projects-use-small-teams)). At the scale of 65 million papers, patents, and software products, small teams disrupt while large teams develop existing ideas ([Wu, Wang & Evans, *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-0941-9)). Social loafing is robust in humans ([Karau & Williams](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.65.4.681)), but it is a motivational effect, and LLMs do not loaf. The classic papers were cited from bibliographic records, so their exact effect sizes should be verified before reuse. The magic numbers disagree (4.6, single digits, 3–9, ≤4 FTE), and only the direction is robust: smaller and less interdependent is better for coordination-heavy work.

Controlled agent studies land in a similar range for different reasons. Google, DeepMind, and MIT's 260-configuration study found a **mean multi-agent improvement of −0.3%** at matched budgets, ranging from +80.8% on finance to −70.0% on sequential planning. **When the single-agent baseline exceeded ~45%, adding agents had negative returns**, and that was the only effect to survive multiple-comparison correction. Agent count by itself was not significant. Coordination turns grew as **(n+0.5)^1.724**, close to Brooks's quadratic. Per-agent reasoning became "prohibitively thin beyond 3–4 agents", and agent-count sweeps peaked at 5–7. Independent agents amplified errors 17.2× versus 4.4× under a centralized verifier ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)). A Princeton-led study framed agent teams through Brooks's and Amdahl's laws. **Preassigned, centrally coordinated coding teams achieved a median 1.36× speedup, while self-coordinating teams ran at 0.88×, slower than one agent**, with concurrent writes and a median of 19 test failures versus 4 ([Mieczkowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12229)). Ensembles tolerate larger N but saturate. Error correlation among same-model agents (ρ = 0.64–0.93) shrinks 30 agents to an effective team size of about 1.07–1.53, and multi-round revision gained as much from 1 peer as from 29 ([Fortuna & Bertalanič](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31563)). MacNet is the outlier, with about 16 nodes "reasonable" and saturation near 100. It passes only refined artifacts along a critic–actor DAG, and its authors warn that agent collaboration "need not precisely mirror" human society ([MacNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07155)). A 2026 paper claiming self-organizing agents beat designed hierarchies ([arXiv 2603.28990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28990)) conflicts with the centralized advantage above, but it was read only at abstract level. No study directly tests two-pizza-sized agent teams, and reports that Anthropic now labels heavy subagent use a "Conway's Law" pattern lack a verified primary source ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/anthropic-says-one-ai-agent-beats-many-subagents-now-that-models-can-handle-complexity-alone/)).

## What this means for harness-system-one

The repository currently holds only a README, so these findings function as architectural requirements rather than a critique of existing code. The table maps each design decision to the evidence behind it.

| Design decision | Evidence driving it |
|---|---|
| Core loop: native tool calls → execute → append observation → repeat, with iteration caps and repetition detection | ReAct's own loop failures; MAST's top modes (repetition 15.7%, termination 12.4%) |
| Persistent typed task state plus an act / ask / recover / done gate before tool calls, applied selectively on state-heavy turns | OODA-Tool: removing Orient was the largest drop; ~2.4× latency cost |
| Progress ledger asking "done? progressing? replan?" each cycle; explicit exit criteria and latency budgets | Magentic-One ledgers; Brehmer's DOODA additions |
| Single agent with CoT-SC or best-of-N as the default and the A/B baseline for every multi-agent feature | Illusion (<10% cost); Interaction Tax; Kim et al. |
| Fan out only for read-heavy, decomposable work: a code-driven orchestrator, about 3–5 isolated workers, explicit briefs, references returned instead of transcripts | Expert-MAS 57.0%→96.5%; Anthropic; Kim et al. thin-reasoning limit |
| One writer per shared artifact; never decentralized self-coordination on code | Mieczkowski (0.88× vs 1.36×); Cognition; Anthropic on coding |
| Deterministic aggregation and executable verification (tests, checks) at several stages; critique only on locatable faults | MAST FC3; Interaction Tax 57% critique degradation; positional bias in LLM verifiers |
| Model diversity over persona diversity, with independent proposals and no full-solution exchange | Interaction Tax +0.188; correlated errors (N_eff ≈ 1–2) |
| Provenance-tagged observations, quarantined memory writes, human gates on side effects, plan-then-execute where untrusted content is read | Schneier & Raghavan; Baya; A2ASecBench as cited |
| MAST-category tracing, checkpoint/resume, end-state LLM-judge evals starting from ~20 cases | MAST-Data annotator; Anthropic eval practice |

Two of these choices deserve emphasis because they run against common practice. First, splitting OODA phases into separate observer, orienter, planner, and executor *agents* has no supporting evidence. OODA-Tool got its gains from one model in structured passes, and every inter-agent hop adds latency, a context-loss risk, and an injection channel. The orientation state should be one shared artifact that every role reads and updates, which is also closer to Boyd's own diagram. Second, search-style methods (ToT, LATS) and Reflexion-style retries pay off only where the harness has resettable state and a trustworthy success signal. Sandboxed code with tests qualifies. Side-effecting real-world tools do not. Several open questions remain unresolved by the notes: none of the critiques has been replicated on current frontier models with native tool calling, no study isolates asynchronous from synchronous orchestration, and shared-memory designs (blackboard versus message passing versus artifact references) lack matched-budget comparisons. The harness should make these cheap to test.

## Conclusion

Across ReAct, OODA, and the two-pizza rule, the transferable idea is the same one in different vocabularies. Performance comes from keeping a clear model of state and from narrow interfaces between separable units of work. Adding thinkers, phases, or headcount does not produce it. ReAct's interleaved thought, OODA's "faster loop", and the two-pizza headcount are each the popular surface of a deeper principle: grounded feedback, an explicit Orient, and a single-threaded owner of separable work. The surface versions all broke down under controlled study, and the deeper principles held. For agents, the human reasons for small teams (motivation, relationships, Dunbar limits) drop away, and different reasons take their place: correlated errors, token budgets diluted across agents, conformity, and error amplification without a verifier. These push the optimum *below* two pizzas, toward one orchestrator and a handful of independent workers.

The practical consequence for harness-system-one is that its main engineering investment belongs in the parts that are not agents: the state schema and readiness gate, the termination and progress logic, the deterministic orchestration and aggregation code, the verifiers, and the provenance and trust layer. Multi-agent fan-out then becomes an optimization the harness turns on per task type, after it beats a matched self-consistency baseline in the harness's own evals. Model capability sets a floor no orchestration overcomes, and the evidence suggests the multi-agent advantage shrinks as models improve. So each upgrade of the base model should trigger a fresh check on whether the extra agents still earn their roughly 15× cost.
